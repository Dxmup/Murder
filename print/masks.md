# Masks

## What to buy

**One bulk pack of assorted masquerade half-masks, 24 or more.** $20 to $30.
Mixed colours, some plain black and white, some with scrollwork or glitter. That
is the entire purchase.

Sixteen go on faces, one is Robin's spare, and the rest sit in an open box by the
door. Assume a third of the room arrives without a mask of their own.

## How the contrast survives a single pack

The design asks for a visible gap between people who spent money on tonight and
people who came from a shift. A uniform pack cannot do that with materials, so
it does it two other ways, both free.

**Sort the pack into three piles when it arrives.** Gaudiest, plainest,
flimsiest. Hand them out from the right pile and the gap reads from across the
room even though every mask cost the same $1.20.

**Then use wear-state.** How a mask sits says more than what it is made of, and
it is the thing other players actually notice over three hours. Most of these
tells are already written into the booklets.

## Two things to check

**Speech.** This is a three-hour party whose whole mechanic is talking in a noisy
room. Half-masks are the right call for exactly this reason. If the pack
includes any full-face masks, do not assign one to a player who negotiates all
night.

**The name cards.** Nothing worn should hang over the chest. The cards are what
make a masked room playable.

## Do not tidy the masks into a matching set

Even from one pack, resist handing out identical masks in a neat row at the
door. The gap between Jules's and Dee's is the story's argument about who pays
for other people's evenings, made in objects rather than dialogue.

And do not stage an unmasking. Nothing in the design pays it off, and Ballot 1
is stronger when the room votes on an identity question with its own faces still
covered.
